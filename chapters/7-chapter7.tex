\chapter{Discussion: The interplay of time, place, and social status in amulet use}

This chapter aims to address the research questions based on the data examined in the previous chapters. First, Chapters 4 and 5 presented the detailed amulet data from the royal, elite and non-elite sites. These included amulet types, motifs and materials by the site, chronological phase and region. Second, Chapter 6 compiled the data into interpretative categories in order to better understand the use of amulets across social groups, phases and locations. In this chapter, we will revisit each research question in order to discuss and interpret each one in relation to the findings from previous chapters. The research questions revolved mainly around the use of funerary amulets across different social, chronological and spatial contexts during the Napatan period in Nubia. A total of 5480 amulets distributed across 548 burials were analyzed, both through qualitative and quantitative perspectives.


Recapitulating, this thesis seeks to answer the following questions: (1) what are the different objectscapes between social groups within Napatan Nubia; (2) how did Egyptian and local cultural aspects materialize in the amulets; (3) if and how amulet use vary across regions in Nubia; and (4) can a long-term evolution in amulet use be identified.

\section{Internal variability}


Amulet assemblages display distinct patterns in each social group, although general practices can also be noted. Regarding the distribution of amulets, more royal tombs contained them than non-elite and elite. Regardless of chronological phase and location, 44\% of royal burials contained at least one amulet, against 21.5\% in non-elite settings and 10.4\% in elite contexts. Although looting should be taken into consideration, these figures suggest that amulets were less common as grave goods in non-royal contexts, especially considering that royal burials were likely primary targets for looters due to their superstructures. 


The distribution of certain amulet types is more uniform. Symbols, deities and animals were the most frequent. The main difference stems from the spread of these types. Overall, symbols made up a bigger portion of amulets in royal and elite assemblages (72\% and 56\%, respectively), while in non-elite burials, they were 49\% of the total. In contrast, deities encompassed 19\% of royal amulets, 19\% of elite amulets, and 30\% of non-elite amulets. Animals totaled 6\% in royal assemblages, 16\% in elite contexts, and 14\% of non-elite amulets. The remaining types were much rarer---less than 2\% each---, with two exceptions: nature amulets in elite burials (7\%) and human amulets in non-elite settings (5\%).

Among the most frequent motifs are the \textit{udjat} and \textit{menat}. The former was the most common motif for the elite and non-elite, while the latter was a significant part of amulet use in royal burials. The prevalence of \textit{udjat} amulets follows a similar pattern attested in other contexts, such as the Nile Delta, Upper Egypt, Achaemenid Persia, the Levant, and Mediterranean; spanning the Third Intermediate and Late Periods \parencites{vergaConsiderazioniMargineSignificato1981}{herrmannÄgyptischeAmuletteAus1994}{herrmannÄgyptischeAmuletteAus2002}{herrmannÄgyptischeAmuletteAus2016}[p.~347-376]{astonBurialAssemblagesDynasty2009}{lópez-grandeAmuletosIconografíaEgipcia2014}{qahériObjectsÉgyptiensÉgyptianisants2020}{arroyoPapelDosAmuletos2020}{arroyoUsoAmuletosComo2021}{muñozpérezEgyptianAmuletsWestern2023}[p.~21-23]{furlanAmuletExchangeNile2024}. Accordingly, the evidence from Napatan Nubia is not exceptional. However, the setting differs significantly from other contexts. Nearly 500 years of Egyptian occupation likely resulted in Nubians not considering Egyptian and Egyptian-style objects as foreign but rather as part of their cultural landscapes and everyday life.

On the other hand, the \textit{menat} appears to have been more connected with royal funerary practices, as their presence in elite and non-elite contexts was very limited. As noted in Chapter 4, some of these amulets were found \textit{in situ} on the stairs of the pyramids, likely during the closing of the tomb \parencites[p.~246]{howleyRoyalPyramidTombs2015}. Although other \textit{menat} amulets were strung together in adornments, their practice of placing some of these amulets on the tomb stairs likely indicates that this motif took another meaning in Napatan Nubia. In contemporary Nile Delta funerary assemblages, they were not a very popular amulet (INCLUDE FURLAN CITATION!!!). Howley posits that their popularity in Nubia may be linked to its association with Hathor, a popular deity for Napatan kings \parencites[p.~246]{howleyRoyalPyramidTombs2015}. At least, one \textit{menat} had a direct link to Hathor. While the majority of \textit{menat} amulets that include drawings or pictures were either plain or featured only a rosette at the base and decorations on the other end, at least two particular amulets featured the head of Hathor. Both were found in the pyramid of king Alamani (Nu. 6), but there was no indication on the report as to where it was located \parencite[p.~58]{dunhamNuri1955} (Fig. \ref{fig:hathor-menat}).


Other motifs associated mainly with royalty are the winged goddesses, human or leonine. Not only were they restricted to a certain social group, but also to a particular gender, title and generation, as they were mainly found in the tombs of queens. More specifically, one was found with king Piankhy, while the remaining were all from his queens' assemblages. Furthermore, these motifs paired with the Pataikos were also from the burials of Piankhy's queens. Although a few examples of both types were found in non-elite contexts from Meroe West and Sanam, they were likely an exception. Taking this evidence into account, these motifs seem to have been only in use during Piankhy's reign, which would make them a viable dating tool for non-elite graves.
